The work is done. The client loved it. The invoice is sitting in their inbox, unpaid, and the due date was two weeks ago.

If this sounds familiar, you're in the majority. As one interior designer put it in Business of Home, "Receiving timely payments is a constant struggle in our business." And the longer a freelancer waits, the worse it tends to get — as one freelancer described it on Reddit, "By the time I actually pushed hard for payment, the client went quiet."

This guide is the playbook for that situation: what to send, when to send it, what to do if the money still doesn't arrive, and how to set up your invoicing so you stop ending up here.

## First, what not to do when a client goes quiet

Before the templates, four mistakes that make late payment worse:

**Don't keep delivering new work on good faith.** If an invoice is overdue, pausing new work isn't rude — it's the only leverage you have, and it's standard practice. Finish what's needed to be professional, but don't start the next phase.

**Don't let silence be your strategy.** Many freelancers delay following up because it feels awkward. As one freelancer put it, describing why invoices slip: "I hate chasing payments. It's awkward. I forget to follow up." The awkwardness is understandable. It's also expensive. A polite, prompt follow-up is not a confrontation — it's bookkeeping.

**Don't get emotional in writing.** Angry emails feel good for ten minutes and work against you for months. Every message below is firm and boring on purpose. Boring gets paid.

**Don't assume it's malice on day one.** Invoices genuinely do get buried, approvers go on holiday, and accounting departments run on their own calendar. The sequence below starts friendly for a reason. It escalates only if the friendliness doesn't work.

## The follow-up sequence: what to send, and when

The mistake most freelancers make is sending one vague "just checking in" email and then not knowing what to do next. Use a sequence instead — each step is planned before you need it, so you're executing a process, not improvising under stress.

### Step 1 — The friendly nudge (1–3 days after the due date)

Short, warm, and with the invoice attached again. Never assume they still have it.

> Subject: Invoice #1042 — just flagging it's now due
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Hope you're well. Quick flag that invoice #1042 for [project] ($[amount], issued [date]) passed its due date on [date]. I've attached it again here in case it got buried.
>
> If it's already in process, no need to reply — and thank you. If anything on it looks off, tell me and I'll fix it today.
>
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

### Step 2 — The clear restatement (7–10 days overdue)

Same politeness, less ambiguity. Restate the amount, the date, and the original terms. Ask a direct question — direct questions get answers.

> Subject: Following up: invoice #1042, now [X] days overdue
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Following up on invoice #1042 ($[amount]) for [project], due on [date] under the payment terms we agreed ([e.g., Net 14]). I haven't seen it come through yet.
>
> Could you confirm when it's scheduled to be paid? If there's an issue with the invoice or the work, I'd rather hear it now so we can sort it out.
>
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

### Step 3 — The firm notice (14–21 days overdue)

Now you reference consequences — calmly, and only the ones your contract actually gives you. If your agreement includes a late fee or a pause on work, this is where it appears.

> Subject: Invoice #1042 — [X] days overdue, next steps
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Invoice #1042 ($[amount]) is now [X] days past due. Under our agreement, [a late fee of X% applies from (date) / work on the project is paused until outstanding invoices are settled].
>
> Please arrange payment by [specific date], or let me know today what the blocker is so we can resolve it.
>
> [Your name]

### Step 4 — The final notice (30+ days overdue)

This one states what happens next, with a date. Only list steps you are genuinely willing to take.

> Subject: Final notice: invoice #1042
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Despite my previous messages on [dates], invoice #1042 ($[amount]) remains unpaid, now [X] days overdue.
>
> If payment isn't received by [date], I'll need to [refer this for collection / file a claim / other step you will actually take]. I'd much rather we close this out directly — if there's a problem, this week is the time to tell me.
>
> [Your name]

A note on tone: every template above gives the client an easy explanation to respond to ("if something looks off, tell me"). That's deliberate. A surprising number of "won't pay" situations turn out to be "couldn't pay yet" or "never saw it" — and the fastest route to your money is making it easy for them to say so.

## If they still don't pay: your options

If the sequence runs out, you're choosing between escalation paths. In rough order of cost and effort:

- **Withhold what's yours to withhold.** If your contract ties final files or usage rights to full payment, you may be able to hold delivery until you're paid. This depends entirely on what your agreement says — which is why the clause matters before the project starts, not after.
- **A formal demand letter.** Sometimes the same words on letterhead (yours, or a lawyer's) change the temperature. This is a common step before anything legal.
- **Small claims court.** For amounts within your local small-claims limit, this is designed to be usable without a lawyer. Limits and procedures vary by location.
- **Collections or selling the debt.** You'll give up a percentage, but a percentage of something beats all of nothing.

*This section is general information from freelancer practice, not legal advice. For a specific dispute, especially a large one, a short consultation with a local lawyer is usually money well spent.*

## Set up invoicing so this stops happening

Chasing is a symptom. These five setup changes are the cure, and every one of them is easier than a single month of overdue-invoice stress.

### Take a deposit before you start

Asking for part of the fee upfront — before any design work begins — is standard freelance practice. It does two jobs: it filters out clients who were never going to pay, and it means a late final payment is an annoyance, not a crisis. If a client refuses any deposit, that refusal is information.

### Bill in milestones, not in one lump at the end

Split the fee across project stages (for example: deposit, midpoint, delivery). The client never owes a huge sum at once, you never have a huge sum at risk, and a payment problem surfaces early, while you still have leverage.

### Put the boring clauses in writing

Your agreement should state, plainly: the payment window (e.g., Net 14), what happens when it's missed (a late fee, a pause on work), and when file rights transfer (on full payment). None of this is aggressive. All of it is much harder to add after the fact.

### Make the due date unmissable

Send the invoice the day the milestone is hit, not at the end of the month when you "do admin." Put the due date in the invoice, in the email subject or first line, and in your own calendar with reminders set for each step of the follow-up sequence above.

### Automate the first nudge

The step freelancers skip most is Step 1 — the friendly one. An automatic reminder from your invoicing tool sends it without you having to feel awkward about it. Which brings us to tools.

## Choosing an invoicing tool: what actual users complain about

Most invoicing tools can make an invoice. The differences that matter show up after you send it: how fast money actually reaches you, what happens when something is held or disputed, and how much of the chasing the tool does for you.

It's worth reading the unhappy reviews before the happy ones, because the complaints cluster in revealing places. In G2 and Trustpilot reviews, for example:

- One designer reviewing **Bonsai** on G2 (0/5) titled their review "Payment Processing is Horrible and so is Customer Resolution," writing that their funds had been on hold for weeks. A Trustpilot reviewer of Bonsai put it more bluntly: "My money has been sitting hostage for 10 days for no good reason."
- A **HoneyBook** reviewer on Trustpilot headlined their review "DOES NOT DEPOSIT YOU PAYMENTS AS STATED," saying over $12K had been pending for six days.
- **FreshBooks** reviewers on G2 raise invoicing issues (including incorrect dates and limited attachment options), and its fees are described there as burdensome for freelancers and small businesses.

Tools change, policies change, and a review from a frustrated user is one side of a story. But the pattern across them is the real lesson: **the complaints are rarely "it can't make invoices." They're about money movement — holds, delays, and support when something goes wrong.** The Bonsai complaints, for example, make more sense once you know the mechanism behind them: new accounts start on a 7–10 business day payout schedule, and ACH bank transfers add 2–5 business days of clearing before that clock even starts.

Current payout timing and fees (checked November 2026 — confirm on each tool's official page before you sign up): Bonsai pays out in 7–10 business days for new accounts, then 2–4 business days once you've built a history (ACH adds 2–5 business days of bank clearing first). HoneyBook deposits card payments in 2–3 business days and ACH in 7–8 business days. FreshBooks usually takes 2–5 business days after the first payment (up to 7 business days for the very first one). Card fees are 2.9% + $0.30 at Bonsai and FreshBooks, and 2.9% + $0.25 at HoneyBook for typed-in Visa/Mastercard payments; ACH costs 1% at Bonsai and FreshBooks, 1.5% at HoneyBook.

So when you compare tools, ask these questions before you ask about templates:

1. How many days until funds are actually available, and what triggers a hold?
2. What are the total fees — including the ones that only appear at payout?
3. Does it send automatic payment reminders, and can you control the wording and timing?
4. What do recent low-star reviews say about support when a payment is stuck?

## FAQ

**How long should I wait before following up on an unpaid invoice?**
Start within a few days of the due date, with the friendly nudge. Early and polite beats late and frustrated — and a prompt first reminder often surfaces a simple problem (wrong email, missing PO number) while it's still easy to fix.

**Should I charge late fees?**
Only if your agreement says you can. A late fee invented after the fact is hard to enforce and easy to argue with. Put the clause in the contract for the next project, and in the meantime rely on the follow-up sequence and pausing new work.

**Can I withhold final files until I'm paid?**
If your contract ties delivery or usage rights to full payment, usually yes — that's what the clause is for. Without it, it depends on your situation and location. Either way, don't threaten it in an email unless your agreement backs you up.

**What if I never had a contract?**
You're not powerless — emails agreeing to the work and the price still document the deal — but your options are weaker, which is exactly why the setup section above matters. Get the next project in writing, even if it's a simple one-page agreement.
